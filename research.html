Research

My research focuses on the design, optimization, and experimental implementation of nanophotonic devices and programmable photonic integrated circuits for quantum technologies. I combine electromagnetic modeling, computational optimization, nanofabrication, and experimental characterization to develop compact, efficient, and scalable photonic systems. My work spans three interconnected research directions.

Nanophotonics for Cold-Atom Quantum Technologies

I design, fabricate, and characterize high-efficiency diffraction gratings and metasurfaces to miniaturize the optical systems used for trapping and cooling neutral atoms. My research includes the development of two-dimensional grating structures for magneto-optical traps (MOTs), with particular emphasis on improving diffraction efficiency, optical intensity balance, and system robustness.

By integrating optimized nanophotonic devices into compact optical architectures, this work aims to reduce the size and complexity of conventional free-space optical setups used in atomic clocks, quantum sensing, and neutral-atom quantum computing.

Nanophotonic Cavities for Quantum Light and Spin–Photon Interfaces

My research explores inverse-designed nanophotonic cavities for enhancing light–matter interactions and improving photon collection efficiency. I develop non-periodic, high-Q optical cavity structures using electromagnetic simulations and computational optimization, including genetic algorithms integrated with Lumerical.

These devices are designed to support efficient coupling between quantum emitters and optical modes, with potential applications in single-photon sources, spin–photon interfaces, quantum networks, and photonic quantum computing.

Programmable Photonic Integrated Circuits for Quantum Computing

My recent research focuses on implementing quantum algorithms on programmable photonic integrated circuits (PICs). In particular, I have worked on the experimental realization of quantum signal processing (QSP) using single photons encoded as dual-rail qubits on a programmable photonic processor.

This work demonstrates programmable nonlinear function synthesis through sequences of optical transformations, establishing a hardware implementation of QSP on a room-temperature photonic platform. It provides a foundation for exploring scalable and reconfigurable photonic processors for quantum information processing.
